# The PushT-v1 detour

Report material. The assignment asks for debugging narratives explicitly, and this
is one: several days of infrastructure work against `PushT-v1` that produced a
green pipeline, a settled throughput number, and **no policy with non-zero success**.
The task was changed to `StackCube-v1` rather than pushed further. This file is the
record of what was measured before the switch, so none of it has to be re-derived
and none of it is lost when the scripts that produced it are deleted.

Everything below was measured on one RunPod pod. The GPU model was not recorded,
which is itself a lesson — see *What I'd do differently*.

---

## Why PushT was chosen, and what was wrong with it

PushT looked like the right task: planar, SE(2) initial states that make a T-II
failure characterisation legible, and a published ManiSkill diffusion-policy
baseline to compare against.

The problem is how its demonstrations ship. PushT provides **no raw
`trajectory.h5`**. It ships pre-replayed into per-control-mode variants at
`obs_mode=none` — actions plus recorded sim states, no observations — and the
episode count differs per variant:

| control mode | episodes shipped |
|---|---|
| `pd_ee_delta_pos` | 888 |
| `pd_ee_delta_pose` | 719 |
| `pd_joint_delta_pos` | 999 |

Every consequence of that fed back into the tooling. The source file had to be
*discovered* rather than named. Any hardcoded "expected episode count" is wrong
for at least two of the three variants, so completeness thresholds had to be
derived at runtime (85% of whatever the source sidecar reported). Dataset reuse
had to be *banded*, because a bare `>= 30` check cannot distinguish a real
700-trajectory dataset from a 212-trajectory one produced with the wrong replay
flag — both pass, and the short one silently caps the T-I baseline.

`replay_pusht.sh` reached 311 lines and `smoke_test_e2e.sh` 470, almost entirely
to absorb that irregularity. StackCube's motionplanning demos are a single raw
`trajectory.h5` generated by one command, which collapses all of it.

## Decisions and their reasons

### Control mode: `pd_ee_delta_pos` — **this survives the switch**

Two reasons pointing the same way:

1. The maintainers use `pd_ee_delta_pos` for their RGB baselines (and
   `pd_ee_delta_pose` for state), so it keeps the T-I number comparable to what
   they publish.
2. T-IV's residual is `a = a_base + clip(Δ, −α, α)`, and α is a physical bound in
   action space. Under translation-only control it bounds a displacement in
   metres, full stop. Under 6-DoF, one scalar bounds translation *and* rotation,
   which have no shared scale — you would need a per-axis bound to say anything
   coherent about α at all.

This argument carries over to StackCube unchanged, with one amendment: the Panda's
`pd_ee_delta_pos` is 4-dim (three translation plus a gripper), so the residual must
be applied to the translation components only. See `CLAUDE.md`.

### Replay flag: `--use-env-states` — **this does NOT survive the switch**

PushT's shipped demos are *already in the target control mode*, so replay only
attaches observations. `--use-env-states` forces the simulator onto the recorded
state at every timestep, so numerical divergence cannot accumulate through contact
events. The alternative, `--use-first-env-state`, replays open-loop from t=0; on
PushT roughly 60% of episodes drift off course and are silently discarded. These
were the flags from ManiSkill's own
`scripts/data_generation/replay_for_il_baselines.sh`.

**On StackCube the reasoning inverts.** Motionplanning demos are recorded under a
different controller, so replay is a genuine control-mode conversion
(`pd_joint_pos` → `pd_ee_delta_pos`) and must actually simulate forward from the
initial state. `--use-first-env-state` is correct there. The general rule, stated
properly: *use `--use-env-states` when replay only attaches observations; use
`--use-first-env-state` when replay converts the controller.*

Honest limitation for the report, had PushT continued: with `--use-env-states` the
recorded action is not necessarily what would have produced the next state under
free simulation. Fine for behaviour cloning, and it is what the published baselines
use, but worth a sentence.

### Backend: `physx_cuda` everywhere

PushT is precise enough that a 1e-3 discrepancy flips a success into a failure, so
the replay backend must equal the eval backend. The backend is baked into the
output filename for exactly this reason.

### Never `--allow-failure`

`replay_trajectory` discards episodes whose replay does not succeed. That filtering
is ManiSkill's, not ours. Training IL on failed demonstrations is worse than
training on fewer of them.

## Measured facts

Peak VRAM across the whole smoke suite: **5237 MiB**.

### Data pipeline

Green throughout, `pd_ee_delta_pos`, 805 trajectories in both datasets.

| | value |
|---|---|
| Source episodes | 888 (`trajectory.none.pd_ee_delta_pos.physx_cuda.h5`) |
| Replay yield | **805/888 = 91%** |
| State replay | 1m44s at `--num-envs 1024` → 38 MB |
| RGB replay | 12m46s at `--num-envs 256` → 243 MB |
| End to end | ~16 min |

The documented "96–98%" yield is optimistic; 91% is what this pipeline actually
produced with the correct flag. RGB is the expensive artifact in *time*, not size
— 243 MB rsyncs in seconds, so it should be pulled off a pod rather than
regenerated on the next one.

**Yield must be measured against the SOURCE file's episode count**, never the
output's own sidecar json. `replay_trajectory` writes a fresh sidecar listing only
the episodes it kept, so output-vs-own-sidecar is 100% by construction and tells
you nothing. This cost real debugging time.

A related signature worth recognising: if a replay output's own metadata episode
count *exactly* equals its trajectory count while both sit far below the source,
that is a `--count`-capped run, not a low-yield one.

### Evaluation cost — the fixed-cost hypothesis, confirmed

- Standalone harness: **65 ms/step at 100 envs**, 97 ms/episode, 0.02 GB VRAM,
  against ~4.2 s/step previously recorded at 4 envs.
- Confirmed with a real policy inside `train.py`: one eval costs **171–180s at
  `--num_eval_envs 10`** and **20s at 100** — the same total episodes, ~8.5×
  cheaper. At `--eval_freq 5000` over 400k iters that is 80 evals ≈ 27 min, i.e.
  eval never became a planning consideration.

Per-step cost is dominated by fixed Python and kernel-launch overhead rather than
per-environment work, so more envs is close to free. **This was measured on
`physx_cuda` and does not transfer to CPU simulation** — it is an open question
again on StackCube.

**Read `evaluate()` before trusting its episode count.** The loop is sized by
`--num-eval-episodes`, *not* `--num_eval_envs`; the latter only sets batch width.
The `Evaluated N episodes` print counts *batches*, so it reports 10 at
`num_eval_envs=10` and 1 at 100 for identical work. This looks like a bug and is
not one.

Consequence for the report: at 100 envs the logged scalar is a single mean over the
batch, so per-eval variance is not recoverable from wandb. Fine for reading a curve.
**T-I error bars must come from a standalone harness, not from the training logs.**

### Training throughput — settled at ~22.5 it/s

State observations, batch 1024, 4.31M parameters. 2000 iterations of pure training
= 89s.

| iterations | training time |
|---|---|
| 200,000 | ~2.5 h |
| 400,000 | ~4.9 h |
| 1,000,000 (`train.py`'s default) | ~12.3 h |

Startup was ~35s, mostly pulling 805 trajectories out of HDF5. 77,365 transitions
at batch 1024 is ~75 iterations per epoch, so 400k iters is ≈5,300 passes over the
data — the curve would be expected to plateau well before the default.

There is **no data-starvation problem** on the state dataset. A
`--num-dataload-workers` sweep was planned and then retracted: the apparent
starvation was a phantom created by contaminated timing measurements. It may still
be real for RGB, where image decoding actually touches disk.

### Environment throughput

State 7676 steps/s at 256 envs; RGB 3835 steps/s at 128 envs. Both are ~30
*parallel* steps/s, so rendering costs essentially nothing here. A "RGB within 2× of
state → investigate" warning in the old smoke test was a false alarm: the 2× is
entirely the env-count difference, not a rendering cost.

### Reproducibility — the T-II prerequisite, green

All three asserts passed: `reset(seed)` twice gives identical states; 100 distinct
initial states across 100 envs; a different seed gives a different distribution.
This code is the one piece of the PushT smoke suite worth keeping verbatim, and it
carries over to StackCube unchanged.

## How not to measure throughput again

Four ways the same number was measured wrong before it was measured right:

- **The smoke test's own printed `it/s`** divided wall clock *including* eval and
  video encoding. It reported 2.32 it/s against a true 22.5 — off by a factor of
  ten, in the pessimistic direction, on the number that drives every scheduling
  decision.
- **tqdm's displayed rate is a smoothed *recent* rate.** It read ~23 it/s mid-run
  and ~2.4 it/s as a final average on the same run, because evals punctuate it.
  Neither figure is the training rate.
- **`--eval_freq 100000` does not suppress the final eval** at `total_iters`. A
  "no-eval" timing run still pays for one. Subtract it.
- **Capture with `2>&1 | tee log`, never `tmux capture-pane`.** Capture interleaves
  redrawn progress bars and destroys exactly the timing information the run was for.

## `train.py`, as installed

Flag names have moved between ManiSkill releases; these were verified from `--help`
at the time and should be re-checked rather than trusted.

- `--total-iters` defaults to **1000000**, `--batch-size` 1024, `--lr` 1e-4,
  `--obs-horizon` 2, `--act-horizon` 8, `--pred-horizon` 16.
- `--capture-video` defaults to **True**. Pass `--no-capture-video` for anything
  being timed.
- `train.py` hard-asserts if `--num-demos` exceeds what is in the file.

**Horizon mismatch, worth a report sentence had PushT continued:** the demos were
generated at `max_episode_steps=100`, but training and eval ran at 150, matching the
maintainers' published command. The policy gets 50 steps beyond the demonstration
distribution. Defensible, but it should be stated rather than discovered.

**Demo provenance:** PushT's demos come from `source_type: rl` — "rolling out a PPO
dense-reward trained policy." That is far less multi-modal than human teleoperation,
which mutes diffusion policy's usual advantage over plain behaviour cloning. It
belongs in any T-I discussion that uses this data. StackCube's motionplanning demos
have the same character for the same reason: they are planner output, not human
demonstrations, and are close to unimodal per initial condition.

## Why the task changed

Published PushT diffusion-policy success rates are low — around 30% in an
independent reproduction, with RGB reportedly worse than state across many ManiSkill
tasks. A low T-I baseline was therefore not, by itself, evidence of a bug.

The decision to switch was not about the baseline being low. It was that after the
whole pipeline was green — data, training, eval, reproducibility — **no policy with
non-zero success had been produced**, and every remaining lever pointed at long
runs rather than at a diagnosable defect. T-II, T-III and T-IV all sit downstream of
a working base policy, so continuing meant spending the remaining time on the one
task in the suite with the worst effort-to-signal ratio.

The identified risk on PushT was also never resolved: that its failures would be
*diffuse* rather than localised in initial-state space, which would undercut T-II
regardless of how good the base policy became.

`StackCube-v1` was chosen over the obvious easy alternative `PushCube-v1` because
PushCube is close to saturated for diffusion policy — a T-II failure
characterisation needs failures to characterise. StackCube keeps a single raw
`trajectory.h5` and a published recipe, but is not saturated, and its initial state
(two cubes, `(x, y, θ)` each) gives a natural and genuinely two-dimensional failure
axis in the *relative* pose: cube separation and relative yaw. `PegInsertionSide-v1`
was rejected as risking a repeat of the PushT outcome.

## What I'd do differently

- **Record the GPU model with every measurement.** T-I reports training time and
  VRAM and T-IV reports wall clock; numbers from different cards do not form a
  table. The figures above are missing the one label that would let them be
  compared to anything.
- **Sanity-check a throughput number against a stopwatch before building plans on
  it.** The 2.32-vs-22.5 error survived because the script that printed it looked
  authoritative.
- **Get one non-zero success number before building infrastructure.** Every gate in
  the old smoke suite was worth having, but they were all built before the question
  they exist to protect had ever been answered once.
